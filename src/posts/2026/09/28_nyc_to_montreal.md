About a year ago, in October 2025, I rode my bike from New York City all the way to Montréal, Canada. It was my first ever multi-day cycling trip, and I completed the trip almost entirely solo.

This post is split into two parts. The first part below covers my experience and the routes I used to navigate my trip.

## An idea is born 💭

I've always felt myself drawn to *challenging-on-paper-but-not-totally-impossible-sounding* things. It's how I learned to swim in the first place: I signed up for a triathlon first, then I found a coach who taught me from scratch.

Sometime in 2024 or so, I came across [a post on Reddit](https://www.reddit.com/r/NYCbike/comments/14z7kxh/bronx_to_Montréal_empire_state_trail/) about a ride from NYC to Montréal via the [Empire State Trail](https://empiretrail.ny.gov/). Even though I'd never done a multi-day cycling trip before and didn't personally know anyone who had, this stranger's experience somehow resonated strongly with me, and I bookmarked the post for future reference. I was familiar with a portion of the trail, having ridden [sections](https://empiretrail.ny.gov/new-york-city-poughkeepsie/battery-inwood-hill) [of](https://empiretrail.ny.gov/new-york-city-poughkeepsie/van-cortlandt-park-elmsford) [it](https://empiretrail.ny.gov/new-york-city-poughkeepsie/elmsford-new-castle) at various points over the years, but I'll admit I wasn't fully aware of the length and breadth of what the trail had to offer.

Then, at the beginning of 2025 right before I quit my job, I thought about all the things I'd want to do during my year off and remembered the post about the ride to Montréal. At the time, I didn't really know how I'd go about completing something like this; I just knew for my sabbatical, I wanted a healthy mix of whimsical and ambitious things, and this ride seemed like a great candidate for that second bucket.

## But when?

I didn't give it another thought until the spring when it was warm enough to ride outside again. I initially thought I could head out in May: the mild spring temperatures, relatively low humidity, and plenty of daylight hours presented an appealing trifecta of riding conditions. But when I struggled through [my first outdoor ride of the season](https://www.strava.com/activities/14223139607) in mid-April, I determined that completing 35 times that distance in a 1-week window only a month later would probably (*definitely!*) be too much of a stretch. I had some races and travel planned over the summer, so it seemed my next available window to complete the ride before it got too cold would be in October. October also happens to be my birthday month, and I liked the idea of celebrating both my accomplishment and birthday by eating my way through Montréal.

In other words: picking early-mid October for my trip was entirely a logistics and fitness-driven decision, but in hindsight, I couldn't recommend a better time of year to complete this particular ride. October is *the* perfect month for many reasons, some of which you can see from my pictures:

- It's likely to be [peak fall foliage](https://www.explorefall.com/fall-foliage-map) in the northern hemisphere, and the trails and trees around upper New York and Vermont are bursting with vibrant colors of the rainbow.
- If you're lucky and don't have to ride through the rain, the temperatures are likely to be moderate: sunny but crisp with far lower humidity than even a month earlier. It's extremely suitable for prolonged riding outdoors, even on those slightly colder days.
- You'll encounter lots of whimsical, funny, and creative Halloween decorations once you get off the wooded sections of the trail and start riding through the small towns of upstate New York and Vermont.

### Day 1: Bronx → Poughkeepsie

[Strava](https://www.strava.com/activities/16046180471) · [Route](https://ridewithgps.com/routes/52581630)

Up until the day I left, I didn't really think I'd actually go through with it.

Well, that's not entirely true. I did book my hotel for the first night ahead of time, so I knew I'd at least make it to Poughkeepsie on the first day one way or the other, but I chose not to pre-book all of my hotels ahead of time despite having all of my routes planned. Even though I'd known for months and had told anyone who would listen about the trip, for some reason, I didn't actually want to pre-commit to anything. *Just in case something comes up and I have to turn around*, I told myself. Subconsciously, I suspected I wouldn't actually give up as it's not really my style, but something still kept me from making any non-refundable bookings upfront.

This would actually turn out to be one of the best decisions I'd make on the trip.

I slept surprisingly okay the night before, got up very early Sunday morning, and sort of went through the motions of getting dressed and eating my breakfast. I loaded up my bike, turned off all my lights and appliances, took a selfie in my building's lobby, rode to the subway, and took the train all the way up to my starting point, the [Van Cortlandt Park - 242 St subway station](https://maps.app.goo.gl/LwQTiNSUUVhKdpw96) in the Bronx.

{% cldImage "IMG_1330.jpeg", "pre-departure selfie", 360 %}

The entire train ride up, I sort of tuned out and didn't think about anything in particular. It felt almost as if this ride was happening to someone else and I was sitting across from them on the train, passively observing them on their way to this adventure. I got to Van Cortlandt Park around 8:45 am and headed straight to the [restroom](https://maps.app.goo.gl/bHaqp51N8oe1xJj79) next to the station's exit and park entrance. It was mostly empty, so I took my bike into the spacious entryway with me (couldn't risk anything happening to it before my trip had even begun!) and tried to rally myself and calm my nerves. *You are funemployed. It's not like you have anything better to do!*, I told myself in the mirror. *You can do hard and scary things. What's there to be afraid of? Plenty of people have done this ride before. It's totally fine. If you get tired or too scared out there alone, you can just find the nearest train and hop on it. This is so not a big deal. You're fine. This is fine!*

Unsatisfied with my pep talk, I walked my bike back to the park entrance, snapped a commemorative pic next to the sign, and set out on my journey.

The ride up to Poughkeepsie was very pleasant, and it was a warm and lovely day for it. If you haven't done it before, I highly encourage it. It's almost entirely on sheltered, leafy trails surrounded by abandoned rail tracks and various bodies of water, with the only non-trail sections being very short crossings of the residential streets that connect these trails. The weather as I stated was near perfect: warm enough for short sleeves, yet never exceeding high 70s at any point. I encountered lots of other cyclists, runners, walkers, and dogs all along the trail in both directions. And squirrels. So many squirrels, many of them assuredly with a death wish of some kind, if their frequent dashes across the trail without any sense of urgency or safety were any indication.

I stopped first around 11:30 at [Trailside Cafe](https://maps.app.goo.gl/8r8qmZ3A8MAnyhmn7) in Yorktown Heights for lunch and a bathroom break, then a couple hours later at a gas station in Brewster for more water. The day had warmed considerably, and I was going through my two bottles faster than I usually did. I got to Hopewell Junction around 3.30 pm. The [former rail depot-turned-outdoor museum](https://www.hopewelldepotmuseum.org/) is only open Saturdays, but I'd hoped to find a running tap to refill my water bottles. No luck there. Google Maps found me [a bar around the corner](https://maps.app.goo.gl/Pz3gk2S2czoJWTXL7), so I went in to ask for some water. Everyone turned around to stare at me as I walked in, clomp-clomp-clomp in my full cycling kit, cleats and helmet included. The older gentlemen were impressed to hear I was riding alone and had come from Brooklyn. Little did they know I had much further to go!

{% cldImage "IMG_1429.jpeg", "hopewell inn", 360 %}

I arrived at my hotel an hour and a half later, checked in easily, and took my bike upstairs with me to get settled in and wash the day's clothes. After I showered, I walked up the street to have a burger at [Zeus](https://maps.app.goo.gl/PuTminJJ4rAH1fyX7) before having a very early night.

### Day 2: Poughkeepsie → Hudson

[Strava](https://www.strava.com/activities/16056471238) · [Route](https://ridewithgps.com/routes/52584435)

The next morning, my friend Tahnee met me at my hotel with her bike; she'd taken an early Metro North train up from Grand Central. I tried my best to fill up with some carbs at the complimentary breakfast before we set out.

The first part of the ride was rather lowkey. We set out from Poughkeepsie on our bikes, stopping briefly on the [Walkway over the Hudson](https://walkway.org/) to take in the panoramic views of the Hudson and upstate New York before immediately dropping into the Hudson Valley River Trail all the way to New Paltz. The woods were serene, with the sounds of nearby creeks and chirping birds providing a nice background melody as we pedaled along. It felt almost otherworldly, as if we were in a movie set in New England (which... close enough).

After New Paltz, the trail immediately re-connects to the [Wallkill Valley Rail Trail](https://wallkillvalleylt.org/wvrt/). At Rosendale, we took the [Rosendale Trestle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosendale_Trestle) truss bridge, which features several commemorative plaques, many of them in honor of fellow lovers of the outdoors. My favorite is this one, [the famous quote](https://fixquotes.com/quotes/i-go-to-nature-to-be-soothed-and-healed-and-to-147025/) by author and conservationist [John Burroughs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Burroughs):

{% cldImage "IMG_1483.jpeg", "a commemorative plaque with the quote 'I go to nature to be soothed and healed, and to have my senses put in order'", 480 %}

I found myself extremely moved by this quote. It struck a chord, revealing something closer to the real reason I was embarking on this trip in the first place. I wasn't actually trying to prove anything to anyone. Well, maybe I was trying to prove something to myself, but really I was searching for something, a deeper sense of purpose and connection after the past six months of my sabbatical when I'd felt unmoored and directionless. Perhaps this is what was missing from the pep talk I gave myself the day before, when I was still gathering my courage in the Van Cortlandt Park bathroom?

We continued past Rosendale and stopped for lunch in Kingston at [Village Coffee](https://maps.app.goo.gl/xJiBuQDAwDuSb5eW6), where I had a delicious bacon and egg sandwich. The cafe had lots of cute trinkets from local makers for sale, and if not that I was still at the beginning of a long journey, I might have picked up something to take with me. They have a backyard (sideyard?) adjacent to the main building with a decent amount of seating, and it was nice to stretch our legs for a bit continuing on our journey.

{% cldImage "IMG_1500.jpeg", "yum!", 360 %}

From there, our ride took us through some small town roads in East Kingston before dropping us into one of my least favorite parts of the trip: several harrowing minutes next to high speed traffic on the [Kingston-Rhinecliff Bridge](https://maps.app.goo.gl/C91YFkuHGUGrnpA66). It didn't help that the already narrow shoulder was blocked off with traffic cones for no apparent reason. Mid-way across the bridge, I decided to stop and climb over the barricade onto the pedestrian path and walk my bike for the rest of the crossing.

{% cldImage "IMG_1504.jpeg", "cars on the kingston-rhinecliff bridge. dislike!", 360 %}

Not long after we landed on the east side of the Hudson, Tahnee's rear tire flatted. It was 2:30 pm, and we were only 36 miles into our planned 60-mile day. Tahnee decided to call a taxi to the nearest open bike shop, which happened to be in Catskill, some 18 miles away. I had a split second to decide whether to keep riding solo and meet her at the bike shop or join her in the taxi ride up. I struggled with it for one brief minute but ultimately decided to take the long view: I still had more than 300 miles of solo riding ahead of me to get to Montréal, and spending an unexpected afternoon exploring the Catskills on foot with a friend sounded *far* more appealing than climbing a thousand more feet on my bike without the company I'd anticipated doing it with. I felt momentarily bummed to miss out on the satisfaction of knowing I rode the whole way, but the feeling didn't last long!

We waited nearly 20 minutes for the taxi to arrive, a super spacious Toyota Sienna van with plenty of room for two bikes. We hopped in, wedged our bikes in the back and headed up to [Catskill Bicycle Supply Co.](https://maps.app.goo.gl/2PZWfsjcZXqo31ew8). It turned out Tahnee's rear tire only needed a new tube after all, but she took the offer to replace both tires and tubes since we were there already. While they worked on her bike, we left the shop to explore the area a little bit. It was a quiet Monday afternoon, and many shops were closed. We passed [the house where former President Martin Van Buren got married](https://www.hmdb.org/m.asp?m=127871) (now the site of a [pirate-themed tiki bar](https://maps.app.goo.gl/u8FQJUwFsSPC58b16)) on our way to grab an afternoon snack. We ended up at an outdoor table at a [restaurant](https://maps.app.goo.gl/cpapN8dhVc9YreyeA) facing the [marina](https://maps.app.goo.gl/uZrQLHkE3wfwRFds8) and spent an hour or so there.

After picking up Tahnee's bike, we navigated the short distance to our destination just off the main street in the village of Hudson using Google Maps, then stepped out to the main street to enjoy some live music over dinner and some very tasty Asian-inspired cocktails at [BackBar](https://maps.app.goo.gl/tG3SSp9ciC7z6oYT9).

I didn't sleep very much that night. Knowing I'd be continuing onward solo the next morning filled me with extreme dread and anxiety. All of the doubts and fears I'd experienced on Sunday came rushing back with full force. I couldn't stop thinking about the fact that every subsequent hour from then on would take me further and further away from the safe confines of my home. I'd be riding the farthest north I had ever ridden from NYC before. And I'd be doing it alone.

Did I really want to do this? Did I really *need* to do this?!

It also didn't help that for the first time since I'd had the idea for this trip, my brain conveniently chose then to remind me that we (i.e. me & said brain) did not have a plan for what we'd do if we ran into any bears on the trip. That was a fun thing to spend several hours frantically Googling while my friend slept soundly next to me. In case you're wondering, yes, there are bears all along the [Adirondacks](https://visitadirondacks.com/about/adirondack-animals/black-bears) and [parts of Vermont](https://www.vtfishandwildlife.com/press-releases/bear-activity-south-burlington-raises-concerns-developed-areas), and yes, they are very much [still active in October](https://bearwise.org/what-bears-do-in-october/). While my internet research informed me that my chances of running into them on my bike was *likely* next to zero, nothing I found could assure me that the likelihood was *definitely* zero, so naturally my brain latched onto that minuscule probability and turned it into a cause for **✨ major stress ✨**.

I found myself once again questioning my decision to go on this trip. What the hell was I doing? Why was I in Hudson, planning to ride another 300+ miles to Canada? What was I trying to prove and to whom? And most importantly, if I *gasp* ditched the trip entirely and just made my way back to NYC with Tahnee, would anyone other than me really understand or care?

I don't think I really had any answers that felt satisfactory enough to calm my anxiety before the melatonin tablet I'd popped earlier kicked in and I finally fell asleep.

### Day 3: Hudson → Waterford

[Strava](https://www.strava.com/activities/16067432236) · [Route](https://ridewithgps.com/routes/52635452)

I woke up the next morning feeling slightly groggy and extremely low energy, but my legs surprisingly had some pop to them which gave me some fresh motivation to do some more riding. I didn't know it yet, but nearly every morning on the road would feel this way: slightly tired from the day before but strong and ready to tackle the day ahead.

I decided to take a looser, gentler approach to the rest of my trip: I'd only go as far each day as I felt up to doing for that day; I'd re-assess my decision at every rest stop, every night and every morning; If at any point I got nervous or scared for whatever reason, I'd just focus on making it to the nearest town; And if I truly didn't feel like continuing on, I'd just detour to the nearest Amtrak station and riding back home; I would not pre-commit to any specific destination ahead of time to have maximum flexibility to bail on my plans, but I'd at least look up accommodation options at my likely destination to see the options available and only pre-book one if things looks scarce. This was my trip, nobody but me was making the rules here. I could do whatever I wanted, after all.

{% cldImage "IMG_1580.jpeg", "entrance to the trail in hudson", 360 %}

Because I didn't get much rest the night before, the 77 miles I'd originally planned to ride to Saratoga Springs on Day 3 suddenly seemed extremely daunting. It didn't help realizing that nearly a quarter of it wouldn't even be along the trail but were merely "junk miles" along town streets necessary to get me to the nearest residential area.  That would also mean I'd need to ride those same ~17 miles the next morning just to get back onto the trail. Over breakfast, I pulled up Google Maps, browsed housing options a bit further south of Saratoga Springs and lucked out in booking the sole Airbnb room available in [Waterford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterford,_New_York#History), a "mere" 52 miles from Hudson. That felt much more doable, probably about 3-4 hours of riding time, leaving me plenty of time to pace myself and take rest/photo breaks.

This of course meant I'd need to make up the distance on other days, but rather than try to squeeze in more miles in the middle of the trip and potentially wear myself out early, I decided I'd have a few shorter riding days up front and save the last day for a big 100-mile push from Burlington to Montréal. I'd done a few century rides before, so it wouldn't be a first for me. I hoped that knowing I had the one final day to get to my destination would give me enough momentum to make it. And worst case, I could always spend the night somewhere along the way and get to Montréal the next day.

The trip to Waterford was fairly chill (spoiler: I didn't run into any bears!) The [Albany](https://empiretrail.ny.gov/poughkeepsie-albany/hudson-kinderhook)-[Hudson](https://empiretrail.ny.gov/poughkeepsie-albany/kinderhook-nassau) [Electric](https://empiretrail.ny.gov/poughkeepsie-albany/nassau-east-greenbush) [Trail](https://empiretrail.ny.gov/poughkeepsie-albany/east-greenbush-albany) was absolutely stunning and one of my favorite stretches of the entire trip, with bright bursts of rainbow-colored trees flanking me on both sides as I pedaled along. I found the trail to be in really great condition, too.

I absolutely loved this day on the trail and could easily see myself returning by train just to ride it again. I encountered a few cyclists and walkers here and there, but for the vast majority of the day, I found myself all alone on the trail.

At first, this made me a little nervous, but once I settled into the experience, I found it extremely peaceful and relaxing. There were large stretches of time where I turned off my music and simply took in the sounds of the forest: birds chirping, water trickling down a creek, leaves blowing in the wind, the crunch of my bicycle tires against the paved trail. It was so easy to get lost in thought, and I'd find myself thinking about my family back in Nigeria, my friends all over the world, the immense gratitude I felt to be able to go on a trip like this, some projects I was working on, the race I'd just completed a couple weeks earlier, a ski trip I was looking forward to. Real life company was few and far between on the trail, so instead I found myself turning to my friends [Jason Bateman, Will Arnett, Sean Hayes](https://open.spotify.com/show/0Yzd0g8NYmn27k2HFNplv7) and [Amy Poehler](https://open.spotify.com/show/1z20EiwuKoDiftKxMVLde1) for audio company.

I arrived in downtown Albany in the early afternoon and encountered my first proper urban riding in three days. What a rude shock to have to navigate around traffic lights and cars again! I made my way to an all-day breakfast restaurant called [Skinny Pancake](https://skinnypancake.com/locations/albany/) and treated myself to a chocolate crepe topped with ice-cream. Yum! They let me bring my bike into their outer vestibule so I could keep an eye on it while I ate inside, which was nice.

{% cldImage "IMG_1639.jpeg", "a tasty crepe for lunch in albany", 360 %}

Continuing on from Albany through Troy, I finally arrived at the junction where the trail forks in two parts: one, which I planned to take to the US border just south of Montréal; and the other continuing west to Buffalo, just east of Toronto, Canada. Perhaps a trip for another day!

{% cldImage "IMG_1642.jpeg", "the trail forks!", 360 %}

The clouds gathering overhead forewarned of upcoming rain. I rode quickly through [Peebles Island](https://parks.ny.gov/visit/state-parks/peebles-island-state-park) and arrived in Waterford just before 4 pm. Most places looked closed, so I settled on the nearest open bar for a steak dinner before turning in for the night.

### Day 4: Waterford → Glens Falls

[Strava](https://www.strava.com/activities/16077820189) · [Route](https://ridewithgps.com/routes/52951350)

I woke up on Day 4 to a chilly, overcast morning. It rained heavily the night before, apparently ushering in the beginning of New York's twelfth season, [Actual Fall](https://12seasons.nyc). The weather app informed me the temperature outside was 54 degrees, a full 20 degrees cooler than that just 12 hours earlier. I looked outside at the grey, misty sky and decided to put on all of the layers I'd packed. I was also glad to have the neoprene overshoes and foldable fenders on my bike to ward off the wet dirt from the puddles in the streets.

The streets were fairly empty, a glimpse into what it might be like to live in the sleepy town. By 11 am, the sun was out again, but I chose to keep my jacket on as it felt breathable and I figured the bright color and reflective panels made me more visible to oncoming vehicles.

I made a brief stop at a Stewart's Shops convenience store around mile 12 in Stillwater to use the restroom, refill my water bottles with ice, and grab a banana. I didn't stop again until mile 25 for lunch at [Old Saratoga Eatery](https://maps.app.goo.gl/sLhKYQgSE64w9A7E8) in Schuylerville. It was nearly noon when I turned in to the surprisingly quiet main street, which may be why the host very kindly let me bring my bike in to the spacious restaurant. I grabbed a table right by the door so as not to disturb the other patrons and found myself chatting with two old ladies at the next table. They were very curious about my journey and asked how far I'd come and how far I still had to go, and they gave me the usual warnings about being safe and whatnot. I had so far not really felt unsafe despite my many anxieties, but it's always helpful to be reminded to keep my wits about me and not get too comfortable as my journey progressed.

After lunch, I continued along US-4. I would not encounter a single other cyclist along the Empire State Trail, and it's not hard to see why: the EST leading north from this point consists almost entirely of narrow 3-feet wide shoulders on US highways. To say they were unpleasant to ride would be an understatement. I thought my radar light's battery would die in minutes from almost constantly alerting me to cars approaching from behind at 55+mph. Never have I been more glad for all my time riding next to fast moving traffic on [Route 9W](https://ridewithgps.com/routes/4544053) (albeit on much wider road shoulders). I have to concur with the EST website's warning that these parts of the trail are _only appropriate for experienced, long-distance bicyclists comfortable riding next to vehicle traffic_. I do not recommend it for beginner cyclists.

I detoured from the trail at Fort Edward and headed about 8 miles northwest towards Glens Falls, where I would be spending the night. This required riding up a [3-mile long climb through the main street](https://ridewithgps.com/routes/52951350?highlight=988-1092) before turning onto an [unpaved trail](https://www.glensfalls.com/business/feeder-canal-trail-12351/) that would take me to Glens Falls. The climb caught me by surprise (should have studied the route more closely!) but the gravel-packed trail along the [Feeder Canal](https://maps.app.goo.gl/T7No9JWjVkHFMWf18) was a welcome reprieve from the miles I'd just spent straddling highway shoulders.

### Day 6: Ticonderoga → Burlington

[Strava](https://www.strava.com/activities/16098740536) · [Route](https://ridewithgps.com/routes/52975366)

I set out a bit earlier than usual this morning intending to catch the [7:40 am ferry from Fort Ticonderoga to Shoreham, Vermont](https://ferryriders.com/ny/fort-ticonderoga/) on Lake Champlain. This is an open air, cable ferry that accepts passengers and all manner of vehicles including cars, motorcycles, and of course bicycles.

It was literally freezing when I left my room, with the feels-temperature hovering around 0°C / 32°F. I honestly wasn't sure I had enough layers on, but there was nothing I could do to change that. It briefly occurred to me that the ferry ride might be rather unpleasant considering the ferry has [virtually no shelter from the elements](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Mzh0hZmIW_c), but I figured I could probably manage the 7 minutes it would take to cross the lake and get warm by pedaling a bit harder once I hit the ground. The motel's front desk staff hadn't yet arrived when I departed, so I left my key on the ground by the main entrance before I headed downhill for the 2.5 miles to the ferry terminal.

Where I was promptly met with a big fat **CLOSED** sign.

*Por que?* Confused, I pulled out my phone, teeth chattering furiously, to double-check I was at the right entrance and hadn't missed the ferry. Yep, I was, and there were no ferry announcements on [the website](https://forttiferry.com/). Well, what gives? It was too cold to stand around to troubleshoot the issue (or even take a picture of the sign), and I didn't see another soul in the vicinity, so I hopped back on my bike and pedaled back uphill to the lodge, thankful I could still gain access to my room. There I discovered via some social media sleuthing that the ferry had [ceased operations for the season an entire month early](https://www.mynbc5.com/article/fort-ti-ferry-closed-2025/66156943) due to [low water levels in the lake](https://www.facebook.com/photo/?fbid=1731982954418202).

Great, now... what?

I hadn't at all prepared for this scenario, and I knew of no other way to get across to Vermont. Continuing on the EST was not something I was mentally prepared to face after the unpleasant last couple days on those narrow highway shoulders.

After an appropriate amount of panicking, I pulled open Google Maps and Ride with GPS on my phone to see my options for re-routing to Burlington. The RWGPS mobile app was surprisingly easy to use, and I was able to easily plot out a new route that would keep me on the EST only for another 15 miles before heading past [Crown Point State Historic Site](https://parks.ny.gov/visit/historic-sites/crown-point-state-historic-site) and across the [Lake Champlain Bridge](https://maps.app.goo.gl/yw3zPxrqC4PBSgZ29) to cross into Vermont. I'd take some quieter back roads through West Addison to get to my planned lunch stop in Vergennes and in the process, skip several of the hillier and busier inland roads on [my original route](https://ridewithgps.com/routes/52585975).

### Day 7: Burlington → Montréal

[Strava](https://www.strava.com/activities/16110698326) · [Route](https://ridewithgps.com/routes/52951683)

It was Saturday morning, and at long last, the big day had arrived. I woke up at 5 am with my stomach filled with equal parts trepidation, exhaustion, and excitement. I barely got any restful sleep despite hitting the sack well before 9 pm, but I was still eager to tackle the 100 miles ahead of me. Something about coming so far and braving some less-than-ideal conditions already made this final stretch seem not that intimidating.

I showered, got dressed, re-packed my bags, pumped up my tires, and double-checked my various electronics before having a quick breakfast. I said my good byes and was out of the door before 7 am, hoping to make it to Montréal before dark. It was pretty cold in the morning but surprisingly a lot warmer than the day before.

{% cldImage "IMG_7989.jpg", "dressed and ready to tackle the final day!", 360 %}

My original plan had been to catch the [Local Motion Island Line Bike Ferry](https://www.localmotion.org/bike_ferry) along the Colchester Causeway from Burlington onto the South Hero Island. It's a fairly popular [ferry ride](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fjFzIk9dmpQ) that came highly recommended. Unfortunately for me, my early departure from Burlington meant I would miss the first ferry at 10 am, so I decided to pocket that experience for a future trip.

There wasn't much life out on the streets that early on a chilly autumn Saturday morning. I made my first rest stop an hour and a half later at a [convenience store on Chimney Corner](https://maps.app.goo.gl/2MirnAeUpt89WbWn9) to refill on water and ice. I then turned onto U.S. Highway 2, which has a very wide, generous shoulder that provided me ample protection from the speeding car traffic. This highway connects via the [Sand Bar Causeway](https://maps.app.goo.gl/WKGxGrt2yQLXaYbT6) (with beautiful views of the lake, including a viewpoint at the [Samuel de Champlain Historic Landmark](https://maps.app.goo.gl/ELNhEPb7EJHnJzk27)) onto Vermont's South Hero island.

Once on the island, I chose to get off the highway and take the longer route around the western side of the island through more residential and farm roads. I really enjoyed this section of the ride and was quite sad to leave it when it came time to get onto the North Hero island. The ride through the North Hero island was much more bustling, no surprise since I stayed on the main highway and it was getting close to brunch time by that point. I made a pit stop at the general store [Hero's Welcome](https://heroswelcome.com/) just before 11 for more fluids and a snack. By this point, I was about 40 miles in. The store was quite busy already, and I had to wait a few minutes in line to checkout. The staff were friendly but surprisingly not very curious about my trip. Even though I hadn't seen another cyclist in days, I inferred that my lycra-clad appearance was likely not an unusual occurrence for them. The store has two hilarious benches outside for people who identify with either of the two political parties in the US. Perhaps it was too early in the day for politics, but neither bench was occupied throughout the duration of my visit.

{% cldImage "IMG_1916.jpeg", "hero's welcome store", 1280 %}

I sat inside for a bit and revisited my route for the day, trying to estimate when I would arrive in Montréal. My math put me at arriving no later than about 7:30 pm, just when it would be getting dark. Would I run out of steam before then? I was about to find out!

I hopped back on my bike and continued north, passing through Hemlock Island to reach the [Alburgh-Noyan border](https://maps.app.goo.gl/VCLCm3P2kALSiJdV6) at 12:30 pm. As I pulled up to the border, I found myself overcome with emotion. It felt unbelievably unreal to reach that point, on the intersection of these two countries, having journeyed nearly entirely solo all the way from NYC. This was a thing that would have seemed crazy to the me of only years ago, who didn't even yet know what sorts of adventures I was capable of embarking on my own.

And yet. There I was.

There was a long line of cars on the other side of the road heading from Canada to the US, but my side of the road — those heading from the US to Canada — was completely empty. I stood there for a few minutes wondering if I was really going to have to settle for a selfie of me and my bike in front of the border sign, but as luck would have it, a car pulled up before I even finished the thought! I quickly asked them to take a picture of me and they were very happy to oblige. I'm so happy I was able to commemorate that moment.

{% cldImage "IMG_1923.jpeg", "me at the us/canada border in Noyan", 360 %}

I walked my bike over to the Canadian immigration officer who requested my passport and asked the standard questions about where I was going and how long I was staying before clearing me to enter the country.

After entering Canada, I continued along Rte 225 (where [VT 225](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vermont_Route_225) turns into [Québec Route 225](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quebec_Route_225) on this side of the border) until I crossed the Richelieu River and merged onto [Law Route Verte 2](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Route_Verte_2), one of seven major cycling routes that make up Québec's beloved [Route Verte network](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Route_verte_(Qu%C3%A9bec)). The trees along the way continued to impress as expected, considering they got first dibs on the leaf changing schedule. All of the road signs were in French (*sans surprise!*) so I was glad to be able to navigate via my bike computer.

This section of the ride felt understandably remote with almost no rest stops of any kind that I could see. My [lunch stop](https://maps.app.goo.gl/f43ZRbaKUdCKCtBq7) was thus one of convenience: it was the first restaurant I could find on the map since I'd left Alburgh some 13 miles earlier. I arrived at lunch just before 2 pm, only a few minutes ahead of schedule. I took my time here, charging my phone and stretching my legs for a bit. Mentally, I knew I "only" had about 40 miles to go, but the accumulating fatigue over the past week was starting to settle on me, and I had to shake it off.

I continued my ride and made it to Saint-Jean-sur-Richelieu well ahead of schedule, at 3:45 pm. This was my original bailout point as there are a few hotels not far from the route should I need to call it a day, but given that Montréal was only another 28 miles from there, I decided I still had enough left in the tank to keep going for another 2-3 hours. I stopped at [Second Cup Café](https://maps.app.goo.gl/8EXJpPFhCwNiG7rM8) intending to grab a coffee for some extra stimulation but treated myself to an ice-cold strawberry gelato instead. It has the vibe of a chain shop a spacious entryway with black walls, which might explain why no one blinked an eye when I brought my bicycle inside and leaned it against the wall.

{% cldImage "IMG_1948.jpeg", "a strawberry gelato", 360 %}

I hopped on the [Chambly Canal Path](https://parks.canada.ca/lhn-nhs/qc/chambly/activ/piste-path) at Saint-Jean-ur-Richelieu and followed it north. This is a truly impressive, well-maintained [20 km long multi-use path](https://www.google.com/maps/d/viewer?hl=fr&mid=1TITaIhjZ1DPoIpBJi222ipP7es-UpZE5&ll=45.342722990596044%2C-73.2475654195979&z=13) that hugs the Chambly Canal and offers a broad variety of scenery, from views of stately homes across the water, to leafy shaded sections in the woods. It was really nice to once again share the path with runners, walkers, and fellow cyclists. After traveling for so long alone, riding through Chambly made me feel less alone, like I had somehow re-entered society from exile and was now just living and doing the same things everyone else was.

{% cldImage "IMG_1954.jpeg", "a view of homes across the Chambly canal", 1280 %}

I made my final rest stop at [Café MJ et Cie](https://maps.app.goo.gl/zugwBw7LrhgzU4Cm7) in Chambly. At 4:45 pm, the cafe was buzzing with patrons of all ages. I glanced inside and saw that there was a line to use the restroom inside, so I locked my bike to one of the racks in front of the café and asked a couple seated at an outdoor table if they could watch it for me. Continuing west past Chambly, I finally approached the historic [Pont Jacques Cartier](https://jacquescartierchamplain.ca/en/road-traffic/jacques-cartier-bridge/) that cuts through St Helen's Island to Montréal. The time was 6:24 pm and the sun was starting to set.

{% cldImage "IMG_1960.jpeg", "Montréal in the distance, as seen from atop Pont Jacques Cartier", 360 %}

To be honest, by this point, my entire body ached and I was delirious with fatigue. My legs were starting to struggle to cooperate, but the sight of Montréal in the distance gave me the extra push I needed to roll down the bridge and descend onto the busy streets. Somehow, I made it through the final 2 miles before stopping at my home for the next week: a lovely ground floor apartment I booked on [Kindred](https://available.livekindred.com/invite/fun.olu) on a quiet, tree-lined street in [Le Plateau-Mont-Royal](https://talentMontréal.com/en/interactive-map/plateau-mont-royal).

I could hardly believe it. I'd arrived! I made it!

I made it.

Wow. Surreal.

My first thought was: *I'm so glad to be off this bike and I'm never riding a bike ever again!* I was that exhausted. My second thought was: *Well hmm, I wonder what the trail to Toronto looks like?* But my overwhelming feeling after all of that was of gratitude to the universe and slight disbelief in myself. What had once seemed a potentially senseless trip when I could easily have taken a flight up, a pointlessly dangerous journey through highways and forests alone, an aspiration at first out of reach until it wasn't... had just happened.

And look at that. It wasn't even that bad after all. All of the anxiety I felt at the beginning now seemed rather silly in hindsight.

In fact, I now know in my heart that this would neither be my last bicycle trip, nor my last solo one.
